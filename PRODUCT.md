# Product

## Register

product

## Users

The primary classroom users are 6–10-year-old English learners sharing one screen and one microphone, with children taking turns under a teacher's guidance. Teachers prepare and publish structured learning content, start sessions for 1–8 children, resolve uncertain recognition, and review private learning evidence.

## Product Purpose

Tim classroom games turn a child's spoken English into immediate, legible game action. The voice adventure board should make complete-sentence practice feel like a low-pressure party-game journey while guaranteeing every child a fair speaking opportunity, keeping classroom operation stable, and separating public adventure rewards from private evidence of learning.

## Brand Personality

Playful, dramatic, reassuring. The experience should feel energetic enough to hold a classroom's attention, clear enough to understand from across the room, and safe enough that a child is willing to try again.

## Anti-references

- Do not copy Nintendo characters, maps, icons, audio, dice-block styling, or HUD composition.
- Do not use 3D spectacle, free-camera complexity, or decorative motion that obscures the current classroom task.
- Do not expose English ability rankings, eliminate children, steal another child's learning rewards, or publicly label a child as weak.
- Do not let ASR confidence, volume, or an unconstrained model masquerade as a trustworthy pronunciation or mastery score.
- Do not let game randomness, long animations, or hidden rules overpower the learning loop.

## Design Principles

1. Make cause and effect unmistakable: spoken answer, judgement, reward, dice, movement, and landing result form one visible sequence.
2. Preserve the courage to speak: uncertain recognition and mistakes produce support or retry paths, never public humiliation.
3. Keep adventure competition separate from learning evidence: children see playful team progress while teachers retain the diagnostic detail.
4. Design for the whole classroom: every child receives a role, an ordered turn, and a meaningful opportunity to contribute.
5. Keep authority deterministic and recoverable: scoring, dice, movement, and events follow auditable server-owned rules and survive refresh or animation failure.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Critical information must use text, shape, icon, and color together. The main flow must remain understandable with reduced motion and usable by keyboard or touch. Feedback must be readable at classroom-display distance, avoid harsh failure cues, and never depend on audio, color, or animation alone.
